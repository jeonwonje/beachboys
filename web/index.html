The problem

It must be cleared daily.

Most of the plastic litter on Singapore's beaches arrives from offshore. Tanjong, Siloso and Palawan beaches on Sentosa are cleaned by about 20 workers, split evenly between manual collection and machinery.

Design report, Section 1.1 and Appendix C.1

The wet band

Sieves blind on wet sand.

Tractor-towed sieves clear the dry sand. On the wet intertidal band, wet particles stick to the mesh wires and block the holes, so that strip is cleared by hand.

Workers sweep debris into piles and lift each pile into a tractor bucket, often in the heat. Above 32 °C WBGT, mandatory rest breaks take them off the sand.

  • Most tiring task. Moving piles onto the bucket or utility vehicle is the most tiring part of the work.
  • Noise. Tractors are too noisy to run when crowds are present.
  • More tractors? They would face the same limits.
Design report, Section 1.1, Glossary and Appendix C.1

Technical review

Two gaps in existing machines.

Gap 1: maintainability

SUTD SAND-E. Its low-cost, exposure-resistant polycarbonate and aluminium guided our material choices. But its collection mechanism is built into the chassis, so it is hard to clean or maintain on the beach.

Gap 2: wet sand

The Searial Cleaners' BeBot. The only commercial beach-cleaning robot in Singapore. It sifts the top 10 cm of sand into a 100 L bin. It was trialled and rejected: it tipped when loaded, held too little and cost about twice a tractor's price. We also expect it to fail on wet sand, which clumps and blinds the mesh.

Design report, Section 1.2

The carrier had to be repaired first

A Botsync Copernicus could not be driven at the start of the project. Its onboard computer would not power up, and the control board's STM32 microcontroller did not respond.

Over the summer, the team replaced the computer with a Raspberry Pi, modified the board to bypass the STM32, keeping only inrush current limiting and the E-stop, and set the Pi up to command the two motor drives directly over RS485.

To prove the repair, it was driven at West Coast Park on dry and wet sand. After 30 minutes of continuous operation with no faults, it was judged suitable as the base for the project.

Design report, Appendix F

Overall concept

Two stages on a Copernicus base.

The conveyor lifts debris, shedding loose sand through its meshed belt. The half-drum then rocks the load against its mesh to remove remaining wet sand. Every subsystem comes off with quick-release pins.

  • Conveyor debris collection
  • Trommel half-drum debris separation
  • Tray and bin debris storage
  • Copernicus carrier, with 4040 payload rails

Stage 1 · Debris collection

Conveyor

As the machine drives, hooked spokes on a mesh belt pick up debris with the sand around it and carry it up to the drum.

Skids
Ride ahead of the spokes and set the digging depth at 10 mm
Spokes
35 cm belt with a 10 mm aperture, and a mesh strip across the spokes for small items
Drive
24 V, 40 W geared motor, through an S5M timing belt at 3:1
Paddle
Ratchets to let debris in, and blocks items from pushing back out
Mount
Two 10 mm quick-release pins, lifts off without tools
Design report, Section 2.2.2

Stage 2 · Debris separation

Trommel half-drum

Flat vibrating screens blind on damp material, while trommels keep their holes clear by rotation. A rotating drum tested in Semester 1 separated wet sand well, but space constraints led to a U-shaped half-drum.

Screen
5 mm mesh. Top half removed, so it takes the conveyor drop
Wheels
Inline skate wheels sized to the rim channel, so the rim holds its line
Drive
Friction drive at 0.24 of critical speed. The motor is calculated to supply 1.8 times the worst-case torque at an assumed 50% efficiency; grip at the contact was not measured
Springs
Two at the midpoint of the drum add downforce
Design report, Sections 2.1, 2.2.3 and 3.1.2, Appendix D

Debris storage

Tray and bin

Separated sand collects on a tray, and debris in a meshed bin at the back of the robot. They are a connected unit that slides out through the back.

V plate
Acrylic, keeps sand off the motor
Tray
1 mm aluminium, about 2.9 L at the brim
Bin
Meshed, at the back of the robot
Pins
Two 10 mm quick-release pins at the rear, with flat PETG pushers at the front
Design report, Section 2.2.4 and Appendix E

Electronics architecture

Two isolated power domains.

The carrier's LiFePO4 pack powers the wheel drives, and the module's 24 V sodium-ion pack powers everything else. They connect only through an isolated RS485 link, so a payload fault cannot reach the drives.

Controllers
A Raspberry Pi 5 talks to the servo drives over Modbus RTU and supervises an Arduino Mega 2560, which drives the motors
Rocking
Between two normally closed limit switches, coasting 150 ms at each end
Broken wire
A broken wire reads as a press, so the Mega stops the drum
Case
Removable acrylic, keeps out sand but is not sealed
Design report, Section 3.2, Figure 15

Intended workflow

Alongside the tractors.

The system is meant to work alongside tractors, not replace them. Their sieves still handle the dry upper beach, while the system takes over the wet intertidal band.

  1. Collect. One worker drives it along the band by remote control, and a second walks alongside to clear jams.
  2. Empty the bin. A worker slides out the collection tray, empties the bin and tips the sand back onto the beach.
  3. Clean. At the end of a shift, workers pull the quick-release pins and clean each subsystem.

What the first session changed

Capstan string will not hold tension. Belt drive trialled and rejected, friction drive on springs fitted. After the second session, paddle flaps were replaced with rubber sheets because the sweeper jammed debris at the intake, and the polycarbonate sheet and V plate were reduced because sand clumped on them.

Field testing · Palawan Beach, Sentosa

The sandpit had no slope.

The wet band lies on a cross-slope, steeper than anticipated, so debris on the conveyor slid back off sideways before reaching the half-drum. The acrylic panels meant to counteract this were not present at the transfer.

The level sandpit could not reproduce it.

User tests and clear-out

5 of 6tasks completed unaided by both operators
5.23minutes to clear out, with two people and hand tools only

Two workers attempted six operating tasks after a demonstration, then were interviewed with the on-site manager. One needed help to clear a jam and resume, the other to drive it to the working zone. All three found the machine less physically demanding than the current workflow. One operator and the on-site manager pointed to less bending to re-collect debris dropped by the bobcat. The other operator pointed to driving the machine instead of spending hours in the sun picking up debris.

Design report, Section 4.2, Tables 11 and I.3

Problem-solution fit

Workflow answered. Collection not yet.

BeachBoy answers the workflow problem but does not yet collect enough debris to replace hand clearing of the wet band.

Design report, Tables 2, 7, 10, 11, 12 and I.3, Sections 3.2 and 4.2

Future work

  1. Reduce transfer loss at the wet band, where the cross-slope tips collected debris back off the conveyor.
  2. Redesign the intake mechanism to improve the field collection rate and reduce intake jams.
  3. Reduce locomotion impediment from the pivoting conveyor jamming, and from wheels digging in and slipping in wet sand.
  4. Design for material resilience with salt and wet sand resistance.
  5. Improve separation so that the majority of sand settles on the collection tray.

These tests should be done on the beach, because the sandpit was unable to provide the slope needed.
